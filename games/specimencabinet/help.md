Every stone is one of a few shapes in one of a few materials, and every shape comes in
every material exactly once. Fill the cabinet so that no row and no column holds two
stones of the same shape, or two of the same material. Every cabinet has exactly one
answer.

**The table.** The pinned stones in the cabinet are clues; they stay where they are.
The tumbler turns and drops the other stones, a few at a time, onto the pile at the
head of the felt. Take one, and more fall in. The felt is your workbench: lay stones
out on it however helps you sort them.

**Moving stones.** Drag a stone from the pile, the felt or a socket and let it go in a
socket or on the felt. Or click a stone to pick it up and click again where it goes.
Setting a stone on a socket that holds another trades them: the other goes back where
yours came from. Right-click or Escape puts a stone in hand back.

**Moves and time.** Each stone set in a socket is a move; the felt costs nothing. When
the cabinet is full, any stone that clashes with another in its row or column is
marked. Your best for each size is the fewest moves, then the shortest time.

**Keys.** Arrows move among the sockets; Space takes the next stone from the pile (again
for the one after); Enter sets the stone in hand, or picks up the stone in the socket;
Escape puts it back; N chooses a new cabinet.

**Euler's officers.** In 1782 Leonhard Euler asked whether 36 officers, six ranks from
each of six regiments, could stand in a six by six square with no rank or regiment
repeated in any row or column. He could build such squares for every odd size and every
multiple of four, but not for the sizes twice an odd number, 6, 10, 14 and on, and
supposed none existed. For six he was right: Gaston Tarry checked every case by hand in
1900. For the rest he was wrong. In 1959 Parker found a square of 10, Bose and Shrikhande
one of 22, and the three showed that every size but 2 and 6 has one. That is why the
cabinet comes in 4, 5 and 7, and never in 6.
