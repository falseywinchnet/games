# What belongs on the shelf

PlaySuite is curated. "It is tempting to include every possible game," its owner
wrote when the collection began. "We of course will not." This guide is the
taste behind that sentence, so you can tell early whether an idea fits and help
the person shape it until it does.

Use it in conversation with the person. When their idea runs against something
here, say so, say why, and offer the nearest version that would fit.

## The four purposes

The collection is described as games for the workplace. A game on it serves at
least one of these purposes and works against none:

1. **To entertain.** Boredom is a real problem and a small game is an honest
   answer to it.
2. **To train the mind.** A little deduction, planning or memory.
3. **To lighten the heart.** Life is often hard, and light-heartedness is not
   only for comedians. A game that makes someone smile on a bad day has done its
   job.
4. **To occupy time without competing with an important task.** Distracting, but
   not too distracting. It demands no sustained focus, and it can be dropped the
   instant the real work calls.

The fourth purpose is the one that turns ideas away. Test every idea against it.

## The tests

Ask these of any idea. A "no" is a reason to reshape it.

**Can it be put down mid-move and picked up tomorrow?** Every game autosaves and
resumes exactly. Nothing is lost by closing the window. A game with a timer that
punishes leaving, or a state too tangled to return to, fails.

**Is one sitting short?** A puzzle, a hand, a round: minutes. A match made of
short rounds is fine. A campaign is not. The longest thing on the shelf is Eggy,
a duckling climbing a mountain in the background, and the owner counts it as the
exception: "It's easy to overdo such themes. So I don't think we'll put any more
games in like that." Motivational themes, long arcs and progression systems are
closed.

**Does it ask for investment the player hasn't offered?** The objection to
arcade reflex games is investment, not movement: Asteroids was considered and
judged "still too invested". A brief timed flourish inside a calm game is fine
(Switchbox hides a thirty-second whack-a-mole). A game whose whole demand is
quick hands and unbroken attention is not. Hazards are choices, never reflex
tests.

**Does it add something?** Spice Wars was built, played and removed, because "it
really just recreates a number of other games that already exist and doesn't
really add anything." A new game needs one thing no box on the shelf already
offers: a mechanic, a character, a feeling.

**Is it a cliché?** "Boring is clichés." The classics everyone expects are
already here, and another one makes the shelf duller. What is welcome is the
classic nobody bothered with: a puzzle that only ever shipped on the Amiga, a
parlour game from an old book, a regional card game. Atom Probe is Black Box
from 1977. Koi-Koi is a hanafuda game most players have never been taught.
Settings can be clichés too: a first draft set in a greenhouse was turned down
as "kind of clichéd".

**Is it fair?** Every puzzle offered can be solved, and the game can prove it.
"Guaranteed to be solvable, but not that every game will be solved." Hidden
information is found by the rules, never by luck the player cannot reason about.
Sound and animation must not leak a secret.

In a game of chance, fair means the luck is honest: dice and deals come from the
seed and are never adjusted for or against the player, and a computer opponent
sees only what a person in its seat would. A round of dice need not be winnable.
Where it is cheap to prove that a deal can be won, offer only those (the patience
games deal only verified winnable games); decide this with the person and say
which you chose in the brief.

**Is the difficulty the player's choice?** The shelf prefers difficulty the
player sets for themselves by how they play (Rock Stack's bowl of fifty stones
lets you build an easy stack or an absurd one) over a ladder of ever harder
levels. When Four Pegs was offered a hard mode, the answer was "we don't need the
hard mode."

**Would someone's grandmother call it cute?** Switchbox passed exactly this
test. Warmth lands. The games people keep open have someone in them.

## Someone is in it

This is the pattern behind every game the owner praised. A puzzle alone is a
utility. A puzzle with a character watching is a game.

- Four Pegs is Mastermind, which is a cliché. What makes it belong is the
  Curator: a formal, butler-like villain who tells you exactly what he will do
  if you fail, laughs when you do, and mutters about being foiled when you
  don't. The first draft of him was rejected: "no clown DNA at all. More formal.
  Like a butler." Then: "not really villainy enough."
- Pen the Sheep is a trapping puzzle from the 2000s. It belongs because of the
  sheep: dozy, clever or cunning, chewing, sulking when penned, trotting off
  when not.
- Rock Stack has a crane operator in a hard hat and a rubber duck at the wheel.
  Neither is needed. Both are why it feels like a place.

What these characters share:

- **They react.** To a good move, a bad one, a long pause, a repeated mistake.
  Reactions escalate: the tenth failure is not greeted like the first. A
  reaction to a pause is one remark and then quiet, scheduled as a single wake
  (see [performance](05-performance.md)); a character does not fidget forever.
- **They have many lines.** A character with six lines is a recording by the
  second game. Write pools large enough, or generate lines from parts, so "the
  same logic never reads the same way twice."
- **Their stakes are theatrical.** The villain threatens something absurd and
  specific. Losing is a maniacal laugh and a set that falls apart, then "try
  again". Nothing real is at risk and nobody is humiliated.
- **They are funny without being cruel.** Light-hearted, with dramatic flair.
- **They stay out of the way.** A character never covers a control or delays a
  move the player wants to make.
- **They are original.** See "What must be yours" below.

A game need not have a villain. It needs a presence: a host, a pet, a rival, a
narrator, or a place with a personality.

## What the shelf does not have

These are settled. Do not propose them.

- Accounts, sign-ins, network play, online leaderboards, anything that needs a
  connection.
- Streaks, daily rewards, statistics pages, win-rate tracking, achievements
  meant to bring the player back. Scores are a top-ten table with names, shown
  when a game ends. Running scores are not shown during play.
- Casino styling, wagering presented as gambling, anything monetized.
- Towers of Hanoi, chess, Go, and further copies of games the shelf already
  holds.
- Anything requiring a long tutorial. Help is one card.
- Real-world brands, logos, mascots and licensed characters.
- Caricatures of real peoples or groups. When an old game the owner loved had
  them, the remake replaced them with a bear and some raccoons.

## What is already there

Check an idea against the shelf before going further. An idea that is one of
these with a new coat is the Spice Wars mistake.

| Box | What it is |
|---|---|
| Solitaire, Spider, FreeCell | Patience games; every deal verified winnable |
| Hearts | Trick-taking against three named opponents |
| Koi-Koi | Hanafuda set-collecting with a push-your-luck call |
| Sudoku | Generated number logic at three difficulties |
| Gems | Match three |
| Nature Cube | Path puzzle across three faces of a glass cube |
| Untangle | Planar graph untangling: yarn, pegs and a cat |
| Atom Probe | Black Box deduction: fire beams, find hidden atoms |
| Four Pegs | Code-breaking against a theatrical villain |
| Switchbox | Find the order that lights every switch, with a girl in the box |
| Puzzle Solve | Turn and flip seven pieces to rebuild a picture |
| The Parrot's Table | Seating deduction from the parrots' statements |
| Liar's Dice | Bluffing against a crew, with stakes the player chooses |
| Pen the Sheep | Fence in a sheep on a hex meadow before it reaches the edge |
| Rock Stack | Balance river stones with a toy crane; exacting physics |
| Eggy | A long climb, running in the background |

In progress elsewhere: Catching Thieves (a Sokoban-like with a bear gardener) and
a first-person maze. Ask before starting something close to either. Retired:
Sticks & Stones, Spice Wars.

## Worked examples

**"A word-guessing game, five letters, six tries."** A cliché; every phone has
it. Ask what the person loves about it. If it is the deduction, Atom Probe and
Four Pegs are already that. If it is words, look for a word game nobody has
remade.

**"Asteroids, but relaxing."** The reflex demand is the game. Slowed down until
it fits, there is nothing left. Ask whether what they want is the drifting and
the vector look; a puzzle about slingshot orbits, turn by turn, might be it.

**"A farming game where your crops grow while you're away."** Long investment
and a reason to come back daily. Closed, for the same reason Eggy is the last of
its kind.

**"My grandfather's peg solitaire board, the cross-shaped one."** A forgotten
classic, short, solvable, pleasant to touch. Fits. Now ask who is in it: whose
board is it, and what do they say when one peg is left in the centre?

**"Shut the Box, the pub dice game."** Rare enough, quick, one screen. Fits if
it has a pub and a publican in it, and if the dice are seeded so a round can be
resumed.

**"A puzzle where you route trains so none collide."** Fits if each level is
proven solvable and a level takes a few minutes. Decline a level editor, a
campaign map and star ratings that gate progress.

## What must be yours

The repository is public and its license takes contributions as part of
PlaySuite. So everything in a contributed game must be the contributor's to
give:

- Characters are original, or old enough to be public domain. No licensed
  characters, no internet mascots of unclear ownership, no celebrities.
- No brand names, logos or product shapes, including as jokes on a wall.
- All music and sound is synthesized by scripts in the game's folder. No
  samples, no recordings, no melodies taken from existing songs.
- Art is drawn by code or generated for this game. Reference images guide your
  eye; they are not traced.
- Rules of traditional games are free to use. Another product's specific levels,
  text and artwork are not.

## The polish bar

The owner's standard for finish is the best shareware of the 1990s and 2000s:
games like Kyodai Mahjongg, where every surface had been cared for. "Take your
time. Work on each game until polished and satisfied." A game that works but
looks unfinished will be sent back. Budget most of your effort for the rounds
after it first works.
